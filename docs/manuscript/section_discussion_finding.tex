\subsection{Principal finding}

The central result is methodological, and it is not the frontier.

Across \ConfirmTotalExecutions{} paired confirmatory executions covering
every resolved candidate, \ConfirmFullSpaceFrontier{} portfolios are
non-dominated and none minimizes all six objectives. That much replicates
what the previous version reported. What is new is that the claim is now
checkable in the way the earlier version implied but could not deliver: the
comparison ranges over the whole resolved space, so a reader can see what was
excluded, which is nothing.

Measuring the difference between the two designs is the substantive
contribution. \ConfirmFinalistOnlyArtifacts{} candidates look efficient
inside the discovery-selected subset and are dominated once the full space is
present; \ConfirmNonFinalistsOnFrontier{} genuinely efficient candidates were
never labelled finalists and so could never have been shown. Those numbers
are small. Their smallness is itself the finding, and it cuts against the
obvious reading: a restricted holdout is not a ruinous design, it is a
design whose error is unmeasurable from inside itself. Evaluating the full
space costs a few hours of compute and converts an untestable assumption into
a reported quantity. At this problem size there is no reason to accept the
assumption instead.

\subsection{What the three profiles actually say}

The profiles behave less like a gradient and more like three separate
questions, and this became visible only once capacity stopped being
purchasable.

In the high-capacity environment the modeled answer is to buy nothing. The
entire purchasable improvement is
\ConfirmHighCapacityMaxPurchasableImprovement{} weighted service-hours across
a decision space of \ConfirmHighCapacityCandidates{} configurations, and a
third of candidate pairs cannot be reliably ordered even at
\ConfirmHighCapacityScenarios{} paired scenarios. A study reporting a
six-objective frontier here without reporting its Monte Carlo error would be
presenting noise as structure. It is worth being blunt: the previous version
reported this profile as having a frontier on which every tested candidate
was non-dominated, which was arithmetically close to unavoidable given how
few were tested.

The intermediate profile is the only one where the decision is genuinely
live. Moving from the profile's own posture to the best affordable portfolio
changes modeled mean disruption by more than an order of magnitude, and the
declared preference weights select different portfolios. If this framework
has a use, it is here: exposing where a preference statement, rather than
evidence, is doing the work.

The resource-constrained profile produces the most uncomfortable and probably
the most useful result. Its declared budget buys a large reduction in mean
disruption and still leaves sustained clinical-outage probability above
ninety percent. Under the modeled assumptions the affordable options do not
reach the outcome the endpoint measures. The temptation is to report the best
affordable portfolio as the answer; the honest report is that the budget and
the endpoint are mismatched, and that a decision-maker in this position is
choosing among options that all fail the stated criterion.

\subsection{On the endpoint that caused the original defect}

The sustained-outage endpoint was inverted in the previous version --- prose
requiring four clinical services, code requiring one --- and correcting it
turned out to change very little numerically, because within this model
clinical services fail together. That could be read as reassurance. It should
not be.

Two things follow instead. First, the near-binary outcome distribution is an
artifact of binary per-step service availability over a shared network
structure, and real incidents show partial, degraded, and staggered service
loss. The endpoint's insensitivity to $k$ is a symptom of a construct
limitation, not evidence that the construct is robust. Second, the fact that
a wrong definition produced almost-right numbers is precisely why the defect
survived: nothing in the outputs looked anomalous. Semantic errors that
change results loudly get caught. The ones that matter are quiet, and the
only defence against them is asserting the definitions in tests rather than
inspecting the numbers.
