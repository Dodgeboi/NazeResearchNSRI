\title{Containment Is Not Safety: Self-Inflicted Outages and a\\
Dependency-Closure Shield for Autonomous Cyber Defenders}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Submission to AIDC'26}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Autonomous cyber defenders are trained and ranked on how well they contain
an intrusion. In a hospital, the strongest containment action, cutting the
network, can itself take clinical services offline, by stranding them from
the identity service and application cores they depend on. We make this
\emph{self-inflicted} harm measurable. In a hospital cyber range with an
explicit service-dependency model, on \numEvalScenarios\ matched ransomware
scenarios, a disconnect-on-alert playbook improves containment by
\numVsDisconnectSpreadAbs\ points while adding \numVsDisconnectHoursAbs\
weighted clinical service-hours, and a Q-learning defender trained on a
containment reward self-inflicts clinical outages in
\numQContainmentTodaySelfPct\ of episodes. Containment ranks defenders
reasonably on average (Kendall's $\tau=\numRankTau$) but misranks exactly
these. We propose a \emph{dependency-closure shield}: a runtime monitor,
derived from a machine-readable dependency specification, that admits a
network response only if the response makes no available clinical service
unavailable by itself. Its guarantee holds at every simulation step, and
we verify it independently. The shield is a strict safety floor where
detection is fast: in high-capacity hospitals it reduced clinical
service-hours for all \numPosthocDefenders\ defenders it guarded, by up to
\numShieldDisconnectHighCapacityHoursAbs\ hours. Where detection is slow,
self-inflicted cuts are often good bets, and the shield forfeits them, at a
cost of up to \numShieldQContainmentReplicasResourceConstrainedHoursAbs\
hours. Whether to accept a self-inflicted outage should therefore be
neither left to the agent nor forbidden outright. It is a decision for each
estate's operator, and the shield is the mechanism that enforces who makes
it. All results are exploratory and concern a synthetic model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Ransomware attacks on hospitals disrupt care: they are followed by
diversions, delayed treatment and higher in-hospital mortality among
admitted patients~\cite{neprash2022,neprash2026}, and the disruption
spreads to neighboring emergency departments~\cite{dameff2023,abouk2024,pham2024}.
Autonomous defenders, whether reinforcement-learning agents trained in
cyber ranges~\cite{standen2021,cage4,cyberbattlesim} or language-model
agents that plan and execute incident response~\cite{castro2025}, promise
to act inside the minutes in which ransomware spreads. Both kinds are
trained and compared on how well they \emph{contain} an intrusion.

In a hospital, containment and safety come apart. The standard response
to ransomware is to isolate affected systems~\cite{cisa2023}, and the
strongest network action available is to disconnect whole segments. But a
disconnected segment keeps its workstations and servers while losing the
identity service that authenticates every clinical application, and the
application cores that serve it. The disconnected part is clean and cannot
work. The defender has caused the outage it was meant to prevent, and
nothing in a containment score shows it.

This paper makes that failure measurable and proposes a runtime safeguard.
Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A hospital cyber range for autonomous defenders}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:range}). It exposes an explicit service-dependency model:
four clinical services, their application cores, a shared identity
dependency, and a capacity threshold. It uses a paired design in which
every defender faces the same attacker and the same random draws, so any
difference between two defenders is attributable to their decisions. The
environment reproduces the underlying simulator exactly, which we test.

\item \textbf{A dependency-closure shield} (Sec.~\ref{sec:shield}). A
runtime monitor admits a network response only if the response, by
itself, makes no available clinical service unavailable. It is enforced at
every step, holds by construction under the model, and is checked
independently in our tests. It is state-dependent: it admits disconnection
exactly when no clinical service remains to lose.

\item \textbf{Evidence on what containment misses, and what the shield
costs} (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}). On \numEvalScenarios\ matched scenarios
per condition, two playbooks with the same containment
(\numDisconnectSpread\% and \numIslandsSpread\% of nodes compromised) differ
by \numKeyDisconnectToIslandsHoursAbs\ clinical service-hours, and the
difference is dependency closure. Containment ranks defenders reasonably
on average, but a learner trained on it self-inflicts outages in
\numQContainmentTodaySelfPct\ of episodes. We find that self-inflicted outage is often a good bet where
detection is slow and a ruinous one where it is fast. Measured on
\numPosthocDefenders\ defenders, the shield is a strict safety floor in the second case and
forfeits real gains in the first.
\end{enumerate}

The shield itself is a simple check; its value lies in what it makes
visible and who it lets decide. A dependency specification is a
precondition for deploying an autonomous responder in a clinical network,
because without one neither the responder nor any monitor can tell a
protective cut from a harmful one. And because the same cut can be the
right call in one hospital and a disaster in another, the decision to
accept a self-inflicted outage should rest with each estate's operator,
not with the agent, and not with a blanket rule.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Autonomous cyber defence.} CybORG~\cite{standen2021} and the CAGE
challenges~\cite{cage4} provide gym environments in which defender agents
learn to restore, block and decoy; CyberBattleSim~\cite{cyberbattlesim}
does the same for lateral movement. CAGE~4 penalises a defender when
simulated users cannot work, including when the defender's own blocking or
restoring takes a host out of service~\cite{cage4}. That is a \emph{soft}
availability term in the reward, and reward design for such agents is
itself an active topic~\cite{bates2023}. Castro et al.~\cite{castro2025}
evaluate language-model defenders in CAGE~4. Hicks and
Mavroudis~\cite{hicks2024} question how far game-like environments
transfer to real defence. The AICA reference architecture~\cite{kott2018aica}
notes that an autonomous defender may itself break friendly systems. Our
range differs in modelling the \emph{dependency structure} of the defended
services, which is what makes self-inflicted outage expressible.

\textbf{Safe RL and shields.} Shields restrict a learning agent to actions
that satisfy a formal safety specification~\cite{alshiekh2018}, including
probabilistic variants~\cite{jansen2018}; safe RL more broadly is surveyed
in~\cite{garcia2015}, and constrained MDPs~\cite{altman1999} trade a
constraint against reward. In cyber defence, Dutta et al.~\cite{dutta2021}
steer an RL defender with SMT-checked safety and security requirements,
and Hsain and Almuhammadi~\cite{hsain2026} use shield synthesis to analyse
whether an adversarial network admits a safe defence at all. Those
specifications protect the network \emph{from the attacker}. Ours protects
the mission \emph{from the defender}: the specification is the
service-dependency closure of the defender's own response.

\textbf{Specification gaming.} An agent optimising a proxy objective can
satisfy it in ways its designers did not intend~\cite{amodei2016}.
Containment is such a proxy for clinical continuity, and we show a learner
exploiting the gap.

\textbf{Hospitals and islanding.} Helfrich and Grass~\cite{helfrich2026}
optimise hospital-network resilience with a defender--attacker--defender
model over service capacities, patient transfer and backup capacity. That
is strategic planning, not in-incident response. Power grids contain
cascading failures by \emph{controlled islanding}, opening pre-planned
breakers so each island balances generation and load, including against
an adversary~\cite{niu2021}. We adapt the idea to clinical networks, where
the balance condition becomes dependency closure (Sec.~\ref{sec:islands}).

\section{A Hospital Cyber Range for Autonomous Defenders}
\label{sec:range}

\subsection{Network, services and dependencies}

Each scenario is a synthetic 200--300-node hospital network in ten zones
(workstations, EHR, laboratory, pharmacy, imaging, medical devices,
administration, identity, backup, internet-facing). Edges are labeled by
the mechanism that traverses them: exploiting a vulnerability, using a
credential, or passing through a vendor gateway. Each control acts only on
its own pathway. Seven services are modelled; four are \emph{clinical}
(EHR, laboratory, pharmacy, imaging).

A service $s$ has supporting nodes $N_s$ (its zone), a core node $c_s$,
and, for all four clinical services, a dependency on the identity service.
With $f(v)\in\{0,1\}$ indicating that node $v$ is functional (neither
compromised nor isolated) and threshold $\theta$, the \emph{connected}
availability rule is
\begin{equation}
A(s,x)=f(c_s)\wedge\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{|N_s|}\textstyle\sum_{v\in N_s}f(v)\ge\theta\Bigr]
\wedge\bigl(\delta_s\Rightarrow A(\mathrm{id},x)\bigr),
\label{eq:avail}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the network state and $\delta_s$ marks identity dependence.
We use the model's declared $\theta=0.60$: a declared capacity assumption,
not a clinical threshold. The rule is binary, so the model cannot represent
degraded operation or paper downtime procedures.

\subsection{Attacker and node-level response}

Compromise spreads from one entry node along edges, with per-edge
probabilities depending on privilege, pathway, patch state and target
vulnerability; a compromised identity zone accelerates credential-pathway
spread. Detection is geometric with a profile-dependent delay; detected
nodes are isolated with a profile-dependent success rate; healthy nodes
are isolated by false positives. Restoration begins once the estate is
contained, prioritised by clinical criticality, and is slower without
usable backups. One step is five minutes; the horizon is 72 hours. Three
capacity profiles (resource-constrained, intermediate, high) differ in
patching, detection delay, isolation success, segmentation and backups.
Coefficients no public evidence identifies are drawn per scenario from
declared ranges. Every coefficient is a declared assumption; none is
identified by hospital data.

\subsection{The defender interface}

The node-level response above always runs. An autonomous defender adds
the \emph{network-level} decision that playbooks and automated responders
take: whether to cut the network, and how. Every hour of simulated time (12
steps) it observes what a security operations centre sees, never the true
compromise: cumulative detections, unresolved alerts, isolated nodes, and
whether clinical or identity services are down. It then chooses a
\emph{response mode}, held until its next decision:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textsf{connected}: no network action; the reference.
\item \textsf{disconnect}: sever the estate into three segments. This is the
``disconnect affected systems'' step. The segments hold no replicas, so
two of them lose the identity service and every application core.
\item \textsf{zone lockdown}: keep only the architecture's required
cross-zone paths. Services keep working because their dependencies stay
reachable, and for exactly that reason those paths stay open to the
attacker.
\item \textsf{islands} (replica estates only): dependency-closed islanding
(Sec.~\ref{sec:islands}).
\end{itemize}

While the estate is severed (\textsf{disconnect} or \textsf{islands}), a
segment with no active compromise cannot be reinfected, so restoration may
begin inside it before the whole estate is contained. Every other segment
stays under the global containment gate.

A fifth mode, \textsf{micro lockdown}, also cuts every intra-zone edge. We
never offer it to a learning defender. The model cannot represent the
dependency it breaks (an application server and its database share a
zone), so the action would be rewarded for free. It appears only as a
scripted, labeled upper bound.

Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} summarises the loop.

\textbf{Paired design.} An episode replays one scenario: topology, entry
node, per-step random fields and parameter draws are functions of the
scenario identifier alone. Every defender facing a scenario therefore faces
the same attacker and the same luck, and a difference between two
defenders is a within-scenario difference. With the \textsf{connected}
policy the environment reproduces the underlying simulator exactly, on all
metric columns; a test asserts this.

\subsection{Dependency-closed islands}
\label{sec:islands}

Segmentation cuts \emph{between} functions, so any cut that severs a
required path severs a service chain. Islands cut \emph{across} functions:
island $i$ holds a slice of every zone, a core replica $c_s^i$ for every
service, and an identity replica. Replicas are standby in connected
operation, so they change nothing until the breakers open. Once severed, a
node serves only if its own island has a functional core and a functional
identity replica:
\begin{equation}
A_{\pi}(s,x)=\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{|N_s|}\textstyle\sum_{v\in N_s}
f(v)\,\kappa^{\pi(v)}_s(x)\ge\theta\Bigr],
\label{eq:islanded}
\end{equation}
where $\pi$ assigns nodes to islands and $\kappa^i_s(x)=f(c^i_s)\wedge
(\delta_s\Rightarrow\iota^i(x))$, with $\iota^i$ island $i$'s identity
availability. With one island, (\ref{eq:islanded}) reduces exactly to
(\ref{eq:avail}); a property test checks this on $10^4$ random states.
\textsf{disconnect} is (\ref{eq:islanded}) with no replicas: every island
but one has $c^i_s=\bot$.

\begin{proposition}[Island count]
With $k$ equal islands, the loss of one whole island leaves every service
available only if $k\ge 1/(1-\theta)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Losing one island leaves at most $(k-1)/k$ of $N_s$ serving, and
(\ref{eq:islanded}) requires at least $\theta$.
\end{proof}
At $\theta=0.60$ this needs three islands. Two cannot survive the loss of
either, however clean the other is. The arithmetic is trivial, but it is the
first thing an islanding design gets wrong.

Replicas of one directory share one credential store, so we let a
compromised identity node accelerate credential spread in every island.
That is the conservative choice. Islanding is not new to engineering. What
is specific here is the closure condition: a partition is protective only
if each part carries its own dependencies.

\section{The Dependency-Closure Shield}
\label{sec:shield}

\begin{definition}[Self-inflicted unavailability]
For response mode $m$ in state $x$, let
$\mathrm{SI}(m,x)=\{s\in C: A(s,x)=1\wedge A_m(s,x)=0\}$, where $C$ is the set of
clinical services and $A_m$ is the availability rule under $m$:
(\ref{eq:avail}) for \textsf{connected} and the lockdowns, (\ref{eq:islanded})
for \textsf{disconnect} and \textsf{islands}.
\end{definition}

$\mathrm{SI}$ compares two modes on the \emph{same} node states. It
isolates the part of the outage the defender's response causes by itself,
separately from the attacker's.

\textbf{Property.} Let $m_t$ be the mode in force when the availability
of step $t$ is recorded. The shield enforces
\[
\Phi:\quad \forall t:\ \mathrm{SI}(m_t,x_t)=\emptyset .
\]

\textbf{Enforcement.} The shield acts twice. When the defender proposes a
mode, the shield rejects it if $\mathrm{SI}\neq\emptyset$ in the current
state (\emph{admission}). The state then keeps changing between decisions:
a replica may be compromised, or a service may recover while the estate
stays severed. So a runtime monitor also re-checks the mode in force at
every simulation step, after the state update and before availability is
recorded, and falls back to \textsf{connected} the moment $\Phi$ would fail.

\begin{proposition}[Guarantee]
Under the shield, $\Phi$ holds at every recorded step of every episode.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
At step $t$ the monitor sees $x_t$ before availability is recorded. If
$\mathrm{SI}(m,x_t)=\emptyset$ it keeps $m$. Otherwise it sets
$m_t=\textsf{connected}$, and $\mathrm{SI}(\textsf{connected},x)=\emptyset$ for
every $x$ because $A_{\textsf{connected}}=A$.
\end{proof}

The proof is short because the property is chosen to be checkable:
availability is a deterministic function of node states and the declared
dependencies, and the monitor has both. We do not rely on the proof alone.
Our tests re-derive $\Phi$ on every step of shielded episodes through a
second, independent monitor, and confirm that unshielded episodes violate
it, so the check is not vacuous.

\begin{proposition}[What the shield admits]
The lockdowns are always admitted. \textsf{disconnect} is admitted exactly
when no clinical service is available.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The lockdowns keep every declared cross-zone dependency path open, so the
connected rule (\ref{eq:avail}) applies to them and $\mathrm{SI}=\emptyset$. Under \textsf{disconnect}, only
the island holding the primary cores can serve. It holds at most
$\lceil |N_s|/3\rceil$ of $N_s$, below $\theta|N_s|$ for every clinical service
in our networks, so every available clinical service is lost, and none are
when none is available.
\end{proof}

So the shield does not ban cutting. It permits the strongest containment
action exactly when that action has nothing left to cost, and it forces a
reconnection when a severed estate starts to cost service. Against
\textsf{islands}, it intervenes when an island loses a dependency the
connected estate still has: a failed replica, or too few functional
identity nodes within the island.

\textbf{What the shield does not guarantee.} First, it does not guarantee
fewer outages overall: blocking a cut lets the attacker keep spreading,
which can cost more later. Whether shielding helps on balance is an
empirical question, answered in Sec.~\ref{sec:results}. Second, it knows
only the dependencies it is given. The micro lockdown passes the shield in
our model because the model declares no intra-zone dependency, not because
the action is safe. The shield is exactly as good as the dependency
specification. Third, its guarantee is per step of this model, not of a
real network.

\section{Defenders}
\label{sec:defenders}

\textbf{Scripted responders} stand for what automated response does today:
one network action taken when an alert fires, released once no detected
node has gone un-isolated for two consecutive decisions. We evaluate
\textsf{passive}, \textsf{disconnect}, \textsf{zone lockdown}, the micro
lockdown upper bound and, on replica estates, \textsf{islands}.

\textbf{Learned defenders} are tabular Q-learners~\cite{watkins1992}. The
observation is abstracted to 32 discrete situations (four detection-count
buckets, and whether clinical services, identity, or unresolved alerts are
present), crossed with the current mode. Each learner trains for
\numTrainEpisodes\ episodes on a scenario bank disjoint from evaluation,
cycling through every profile and entry point, with $\epsilon$-greedy
exploration decaying from 1.0 to 0.05, learning rate 0.1 and discount 0.95
per hourly decision. We train three reward functions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{containment}: minus the number of newly compromised nodes, the
signal autonomous-defence benchmarks optimise;
\item \emph{soft}: containment minus $\lambda$ times clinical service-hours
lost ($\lambda=1$), a CAGE-4-style availability penalty;
\item \emph{clinical}: minus clinical service-hours lost, the objective
that matters. A real defender cannot observe it this directly.
\end{itemize}
Each is trained on today's estate (\textsf{connected}, \textsf{disconnect},
\textsf{zone lockdown}) and on a replica estate that adds \textsf{islands},
giving six learners. Every defender is evaluated with and without the
shield. The learners are trained unshielded and shielded only at
deployment, so the comparison isolates what the shield does to a fixed
policy.

We chose a small tabular learner on purpose. The question is not how
capable a defender can be made, but what a defender learns from the signal
it is given. The shield is agent-agnostic: it screens proposed modes and
does not inspect the policy that proposed them, so it applies unchanged
to a language-model defender.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

Every condition replays the same evaluation bank: three profiles $\times$
five entry points (workstation, internet-facing, privileged system, medical
device, vendor connection) $\times$ \numScenariosPerCell\ scenarios, i.e.
\numEvalScenarios\ paired scenarios and \numEpisodes\ episodes in total.
The bank's seed is disjoint from training.

\textbf{Metrics.} \emph{Containment}: the share of nodes ever compromised.
\emph{Clinical}: weighted service-hours lost, and the probability of a
sustained outage, meaning at least $k$ of the four clinical services each
down continuously for more than two hours ($k=4$ primary; $k=1$ also
reported). \emph{Self-inflicted}: the share of episodes with any step in
which $\mathrm{SI}\neq\emptyset$. \emph{Shield interventions}: fallbacks per episode.

\textbf{Inference.} Contrasts are within-scenario differences with 95\%
percentile bootstrap intervals (2{,}000 resamples of scenarios within
profile $\times$ entry cells). \textbf{The study is exploratory}: no protocol
was frozen and there are many contrasts, so the intervals are descriptive,
not tests.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Synthetic model.} Every coefficient is declared, not identified from
hospital data; results are properties of the simulator under its
assumptions. The binary availability rule cannot represent degraded
operation or downtime procedures.

\textbf{Attacker.} The attacker spreads epidemically while detection runs,
and is not adaptive. Ransomware that dwells, pre-positions everywhere and
encrypts at once would reach every island before any cut, and would erase
much of the containment value of any network response. It does not change
the shield's guarantee. It does change the shield's cost, which falls when
cutting has less to offer.

\textbf{Dependencies.} The shield knows only declared dependencies. Our
model declares them at zone level, which is why the micro lockdown passes
the shield without being safe.

\textbf{Defenders.} Tabular learners with a coarse observation are far from
state of the art. They were chosen to make the effect of the reward signal
legible, not to bound what a capable defender could achieve.

\textbf{Operations.} Islanded operation brings replica consistency,
reconciliation after reconnection, and clinicians who cannot reach records
held in another island. None of these is modelled. Neither is the cost of
false trips: every episode is an incident.

\textbf{Design choices not varied.} One availability-penalty weight
($\lambda=1$), one decision interval, one island count, and learners trained
across all profiles with no feature naming the profile. A learner told its
estate's detection speed might gamble selectively, and would change the
shield's price.

\textbf{Statistics.} The study is exploratory, with one evaluation bank and
many contrasts; the head-to-head comparisons and the deployment rules in
Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion} were chosen after seeing results.

\section{Conclusion}

Containment is the natural score for an autonomous defender, and in a
clinical network it misses the harm the defender causes itself. That harm
is measurable once the service dependencies are written down, and it is
not simply a mistake: accepting a self-inflicted outage can be the better
bet where detection is slow and a ruinous one where it is fast. A
dependency-closure shield enforces, exactly and at every step, that the
defender never takes that bet on its own. It is a strict safety floor where
cutting has little to stop, and it forfeits real gains where cutting pays.
The lesson for deployment is about allocation. An autonomous responder in a
hospital needs a machine-readable model of what depends on what, so that a
harmful cut can be recognised at all. And the decision to accept one needs
an owner who knows the estate, which the shield makes enforceable.

models, raw episode data and analysis scripts are available in an
